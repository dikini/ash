DN-0055: Algebra of Regions, Lifetimes, and Ownership

Development design notes · ash-lang 67c79dd

Informative design work. Proposals and experiments may be incomplete or superseded. Read the language specification for current rules.

All design notes
  • ID: DN-0055
  • Status: exploring
  • Related: DN-0032, DN-0035, DN-0054, DN-0053, DN-0051, generic cell checkpoint, DN-0038, DN-0013

This design note is informative. It records candidate definitions, elementary derivations, and open proof obligations. It defines no current Ash semantics, accepts no architecture, and authorizes no implementation or prototype. No complete algebraic soundness, memory-safety, or compiler-correctness theorem is claimed.

Question

Can an algebra connect region placement and allocation with ownership, lifetimes, and reclamation while keeping those concepts distinct? Which laws follow from definitions, which depend on a region type or ownership scheme, and which require a proof of observable program equivalence?

The target includes a shared base of constraints with protocol-dependent variations. Each protocol specializes that base and must preserve it; it does not waive shared validity, authority-provenance, or reclamation obligations.

The working candidate combines a lifetime order, placement facts, restricted ownership contexts, and typed state transitions. Lexical scope is the first ownership scheme; a fixed arena with bulk reclamation is the first storage and reclamation composition.

The lexical-region product supplies default placement, explicit selection, lexical ownership, bulk reclamation, stored closures and recursive immutable graphs. The specification governs that base; the finite model evidence records its separate bounded preservation result and assumptions. The broader permission, channel and alternative-ownership explorations below remain informative future work, not completed product behavior or additional M2 prerequisites. Usage- and lifetime-based placement inference remains a later optimization, not a substitute for the implemented default. DN-0054 retains related composition questions.

A future comparison considers sequential processes communicating through dedicated channels. It tests ownership across communication without requiring shared mutable storage. It remains separate from the first sequential proof model and defines no current concurrency facility.

Context

The specification governs current Ash. Its resource rules track dependencies through values, captures, selected evidence, and operation metadata. Execution separates non-owning access from scope-owned completion. Capture and resume move the same owner; checked discard completes owned abandoned scopes. These are constraints on a future region algebra, not a general implemented region interface or a proof of this candidate.

DN-0032 derives bounded sequencing and context-composition laws, with conditional scope movement and counterexamples. Its context product and lifetime envelope do not merge storage regions, select their allocators, or transfer their ownership.

DN-0035 and DN-0054 record the direction:

  • Regions provide placement and allocation.
  • An ownership scheme determines who owns a region and its lifetime. Lexical scope is the first scheme, among other possible schemes.
  • Region ownership includes its allocated objects. Valid object use requires the region to remain valid; choosing storage cannot shorten a required use.
  • Region types may specify different object reclamation policies. Individual objects may be reclaimed earlier; reclaiming the region reclaims all remaining objects en masse, after required completion.
  • Mutation and access permissions remain separate. A cell can use region storage without placement determining its logical access lifetime.

The generic cell checkpoint is implemented and integrated into local Main; its original accepted proposal is retained in Git history. Its strict retained-payload dependencies and resource-scope cell lifetime remain constraints. This note changes neither current cells nor their evidence.

DN-0035's later process comparison starts with isolated storage and requires transport to establish receiver-owned data. DN-0051 separates task ownership, suspension, and parallel sharing contracts. Neither supplies an implemented channel or proof of safe message transport. Their distinction between copying, sharing, and ownership transfer governs the comparison below.

DN-0038 separates operation permissions, alias authority, value usage, and completion. Current cells permit mediated reads and writes through reusable aliases; their scope owns release. The polarity comparison below proposes additional access facts, not a reinterpretation of those handles as exclusive references.

Constraints

  • Begin with a sequential execution model and lexical ownership. Include suspended owned continuations; inactivity does not end their lifetimes.
  • Separate usable lifetime, resident object storage, and backing protection. Source completion need not establish physical reclamation.
  • Preserve region and object identities through aliasing. Model names do not imply source-visible addresses, runtime registries, or heap inspection.
  • Keep completion authority distinct from reusable access. Copying a handle must not create another owner or another permission to reclaim storage.
  • Distinguish ordinary object-reference graphs, which may be cyclic, from resource-owner dependency graphs constrained by the ownership scheme.
  • Preserve request-payload boundaries and checked construction authority. Lifetime inequalities alone cannot authorize an outward payload transfer.
  • Account for allocation failure, partial construction, retained backing, cleanup, and observable operation order before claiming execution laws.
  • Separate common preservation constraints from protocol-specific ownership construction, access compatibility, lifetime schemes, and completion rules. Do not promote one protocol's exclusion or phase rule into a base axiom.
  • Keep other ownership schemes, owner transport, moving collection, concurrency, usage/lifetime-based placement inference, and source proof syntax outside the bounded lexical proof model. The specification supplies the product's default/explicit placement contract; that model's correspondence and limits remain recorded separately. The channel comparison below explores additional obligations; it does not enlarge that model's established evidence or authorize execution.

Candidate directions

Comparison and working choice

Approach Useful property Limit
Lifetime constraints alone Express outlives relations and bounds on use. Do not establish placement, reclamation authority, initialization, or required completion.
Typed transitions with lifetime and ownership contexts State how allocation and management preserve validity; reuse bounded sequencing arguments. Need a precise state, operation premises, and a preservation proof.
A general permission algebra for every ownership scheme Could expose common structure across later schemes. Premature before a second scheme supplies concrete obligations and contrary cases.

Use the second approach first, with the lifetime order as one component. Extend its ownership rules only when another named scheme supplies a contract. This is a working choice for exploration, not an accepted architecture.

Base constraints and protocol variations

Use a shared base of constraints and explicit protocol-dependent refinements. The following is a candidate base to test against the comparisons in this note, not a proved minimal, independent, or complete axiom set:

Candidate base constraint Protocol-dependent variation
Ownership and authority facts have a justified construction or transition origin. Aliasing a name or observing an operation does not create them. How storage and polarity ownership are established, partitioned, shared, borrowed, delegated, transferred, or recombined.
Every admitted use is covered by its required authority, usable validity, backing protection, and actual retained dependencies. Lexical ownership, transportable regions, read leases, task or channel support, and the extents of their lifetimes.
Operations preserve admitted representation, initialization, payload, and access invariants. Compatible reader/writer configurations, simultaneous or alternating access, publication, atomics, locks, or other mediation.
Facts remain attached to the correct identities, actors, footprints, and operation extents. Ownership or access authority is not inferred solely from equal lifetimes or shared placement. Which actors own which polarities, which grants coexist, and whether a protocol uses changing phases.
Declared ownership and completion obligations remain accounted for through every transition. Nonduplicable authority is not duplicated; required completion is not lost. Unique, joint, or otherwise specified polarity ownership; closure, cancellation, failure, return, and completion policies.
Reclamation cannot invalidate an admitted use or required completion. Reclaiming a region releases all remaining resident objects collectively. Early object collection, bulk-only reclamation, lease settlement, draining, deferred reclamation, and retained backing.
An operation respects the layout, execution-domain, and visibility constraints applicable to its support. Required alignment, affinity, memory-order guarantees, backing policy, and locality preferences.

The base constrains the relationship between storage and polarity ownership without equating them. A region's owner owns its resident object storage, while other actors may own the read and write polarities. Their rules must jointly preserve the required uses of the objects. The base does not impose one reader, one writer, exclusive mutation, alternating phases, or lexical lifetime equality for every ownership scheme.

For protocol Pi, write B(S) for the common state constraints and V_Pi(S) for its additional invariant, including any instantiation of shared parameters. Its well-formed states satisfy:

WellFormed_Pi(S) = B(S) and V_Pi(S)

The protocol supplies its establishment and transition rules. Establishment must produce a well-formed state from justified input authority and support. For every admitted step from S to S_after, the preservation obligation is:

WellFormed_Pi(S) implies WellFormed_Pi(S_after)

This implication is a proof target conditional on the particular admitted transition and its premises, not a claim that every state change is valid. Include failure, partial construction, cancellation, closure, and deferred completion steps; committed effects need not roll back. Protocol refinements may strengthen or instantiate the base but cannot waive its guarantees.

For example, one access protocol can permit overlapping read and write polarity owners and another can require alternating producer and consumer access. Both must keep each admitted access authorized, its dependencies valid, and its storage protected. Those preservation constraints are common; exclusion and phase choices are protocol variations.

Composition requires more than independently valid protocols. State the mapping of shared storage, polarities, grants, and obligations. Show that local steps and cross-protocol handoffs preserve the combined constraints. A channel that allows retained reads and a pool that reuses bytes immediately on receipt have incompatible assumptions unless their composition establishes another admitted handoff or lifetime contract. Conjoining invariant names is not that proof.

Progress, fairness, eventual completion, and quantitative bounds require their own protocol and execution assumptions. State preservation alone establishes none of them. Further comparisons may reveal missing or redundant base constraints; record that evidence before treating a candidate as a common law.

Process-calculus and Links comparisons

Primary sources checked on 2026-10-19 supply comparison techniques and known boundaries. This is a focused reading map, not a complete literature review or an adoption of their calculi. The interpretations for Ash in the last column are proposed transfer questions, not results established by the cited work.

Choose observations before claiming behavioral refinement or equivalence. Well-formed ownership states alone cannot distinguish refusal, deadlock, or internal divergence.

Ordinary pi-calculus has input/output action polarity and name mobility, but does not impose an ownership handover merely because a name is sent. In a schematic ordinary communication step, sending a on c substitutes it for the receiver's bound name x:

c<a>.P | c(x).Q  ->  P | Q{a/x}

The sender's continuation P may still use a; nothing in this rule consumes its input or output authority. Thus name possession and scope can express accessibility without separately tracking ownership of each polarity. I/O capability typing restricts the directions available to the receiver. Linear and session disciplines add rules for distributing, consuming, or delegating authority; their particular context rules determine which rights the sender may retain. This example explains the cited calculi, not Ash syntax or a new Ash transition. Their polarity treatment must be examined at the chosen typed variant rather than assumed to be an ownership discipline of plain pi-calculus.

Three further distinctions matter for the base constraints:

  • State refinement by B(S) and V_Pi(S) differs from behavioral refinement in a chosen observation model. Connecting them requires a semantics and proof; invariant conjunction does not establish a CSP refinement result.
  • Channel direction, endpoint usage, name scope, and ownership of backing are different facts. Endpoint transport must preserve the relevant relationships without treating name extrusion as physical storage lifetime extension.
  • Session fidelity, alias freedom, deadlock freedom, and valid reclamation are different properties. The cited calculi establish particular combinations under particular assumptions, not a single guarantee attached to the phrase session typed. No result here establishes zero-copy, affinity, or allocator correctness for Ash.

Useful later stress cases include transporting one owned polarity while its complement stays with another actor; cancelling a message carrying delegated authority; and capturing a continuation that retains that authority. A shared access-point comparison should test preservation separately from progress. These extend beyond the first data-only dedicated-channel case and require their own bounded assignment before executable work.

DN-0013 already uses session-like protocols to evaluate general Ash mechanisms without selecting a privileged session calculus. Reuse that comparison boundary: identify which facts need checking before choosing source syntax, type-system machinery, or a runtime representation.

Lifetime order

For one execution, let a lifetime be a set of event positions at which the corresponding validity holds. Define a <= b by set inclusion. Source checking would need to establish its constraints for every admitted execution, not just one observed run. Lifetime extents include suspension where the same ownership remains valid.

The following are elementary consequences of set inclusion. Equality here is equality of lifetime extents; two different owners or regions can have equal extents without having equal identities:

a <= a
a <= b and b <= c  implies  a <= c
a <= b and b <= a  implies  a = b

Intersection is the greatest common bound. Its laws follow from membership in both operand sets:

a intersect b <= a
a intersect b <= b
c <= a and c <= b  implies  c <= a intersect b

a intersect b = b intersect a
(a intersect b) intersect c = a intersect (b intersect c)
a intersect a = a

This mathematical domain does not imply that every set, intersection, or union can be expressed by an Ash scope. Nor does containing two lifetimes in an enclosing interval authorize moving acquisition or completion. The interval envelope in DN-0032 is an analysis bound, not a storage-region fusion rule.

For a fixed execution event set E, the full domain of lifetime sets forms a complete lattice: intersection is meet, union is join, the empty set is bottom, and E is top. Expressible lexical lifetimes need not be closed under union; an enclosing scope gives an upper bound rather than necessarily that exact union. This lifetime lattice does not establish a lattice of placements, owners, ownership schemes, or reclamation policies. Those would need their own orders and proofs that the required bounds exist.

Placement, ownership, and usable lifetime

Use r for a region, x for an allocated object, and o for an owner. place(x) = r records placement. Own(o, r) records ownership of the region and responsibility for its resident object storage. This ownership alone grants no additional mutation, resource access, or early-reclamation operation.

An ownership scheme supplies the rules establishing Own and region lifetime. For the first scheme, a successful region-owning scope activation s provides:

Own(s, r)
region_lifetime(r) = owner_scope_lifetime(s)

These equalities describe logical availability under lexical ownership. Retained or static backing may physically remain longer. Copying an access value preserves placement and ownership; it does not copy Own authority. Deep copying, relocation, and ownership transfer would require separate rules.

For each object define required_use(x) from its ownership and access contracts, and valid(x) as its usable lifetime. Required use includes uses and pending completion justified by those contracts, not merely accesses that happened to execute. The candidate preservation condition is:

required_use(x) <= valid(x) <= region_lifetime(place(x))
region_lifetime(r) <= backing_protection(r)

The first inequality prevents premature loss of required validity. The second bounds object use by its region. The last permits protected backing after ordinary access ends without granting a longer usable lifetime.

These are obligations to establish, not theorems obtained by naming a region. Once placement is fixed, an escaping use rejects rather than silently extending the owner scope or shortening the object's required lifetime. A later placement-inference optimization may instead select an existing enclosing region, as explored below; this does not replace M2's approved closest-live default. Other schemes must establish the same validity relationship under their own owner rules.

Establishing and discovering ownership

The ownership scheme establishes Own(o, r); equal lifetimes or lexical visibility alone cannot establish it. A checker then propagates that fact through region access and object placement. Allocating into r puts the new object under its established owner's collective storage ownership. The helper executing the construction does not acquire ownership merely by allocating. Aliasing and borrowing preserve that relationship. Ownership transfer would require a separate scheme rule.

For the later inference-optimization comparison, consider a variant where each entered lexical scope activation s successfully establishes one region r_s, owned by s and available for that activation's lifetime. This is a candidate variant, not the implemented default behavior or a replacement for its call-boundary contract. Ordinary blocks and calls acquire no region; explicit selection scopes new construction without moving existing values. This variant selects no physical representation or source annotation syntax.

The three annotation variations constrain the same relationships:

Variation Established facts and remaining inference
No placement or owner annotation Each scope establishes its region's owner and lifetime. Construction defaults locally; usage requirements can require an enclosing region, whose owner then owns the object's storage.
Object annotated with region r Placement selects r; its established owner supplies collective storage ownership. The region must be accessible with allocation authority, and dependencies still bound usable lifetime.
Object annotated with storage owner o Placement must select a region r for which Own(o, r) holds. One region per scope determines it; multiple regions per owner require another selection rule.

An owner annotation here denotes storage ownership, not merely the object that holds a reference. Neither annotation by itself promises usable validity for the owner's entire lifetime. If that stronger promise is required, all retained dependencies must support it. Region and owner annotations constrain inference; conflicting requirements need a diagnostic rather than silent retargeting.

Dependency bounds and projection

Let deps(v) describe the storage and capabilities required by the particular value or access being checked. It follows actual retained fields, captures, evidence, and operation metadata. An unused environment entry alone is not a dependency. Bounds on usable lifetime take the following form:

valid(v) <= storage_validity(v)
            intersect intersection(valid(d) for d in deps(v))

Independent ordinary values add no capability dependency. A closure allocated in outer region A that reads scoped cell C still requires C; the closure's longer-lived storage cannot restore cell access after C closes.

For a worked derivation, suppose B is lexically owned inside A, and y is allocated in B while retaining an existing reference into A:

region_lifetime(B) <= region_lifetime(A)

valid(y) <= region_lifetime(B) intersect region_lifetime(A)
         = region_lifetime(B)

The intersection equality follows from inclusion. This derives a bound on y, not an escape permission. Projecting the existing outer reference can remove the dependency on B if the result needs no storage in B. A view into an inline field would retain that dependency instead.

The current generic cell contract remains stricter for retained mutable payloads: their resource owners must strictly outlive the cell owner. The intersection calculation does not admit temporary younger storage, self-capture, or cycles of cell owners. Ordinary immutable graph cycles are a different relation and remain possible. Likewise, constructing the closure above cannot send a younger-dependent payload to an outer interpreter. DN-0054's reservation and local initialization boundary remains a separate construction obligation.

Access polarity and lifetime-indexed permissions

Read and write polarities are facets whose authority can belong to different actors. Keep their ownership distinct from storage ownership and from the individual accesses admitted under that authority. Channel endpoint direction and positive or negative type occurrence are related comparisons, not identical concepts. A polarity label alone establishes none of these ownership facts.

Ownership of polarities

Use OwnPolarity_S(a, x, p) for actor a owning polarity p of object or footprint x in state S. The candidate distinguishes these simultaneous facts, with R and W denoting read and write polarities:

OwnPolarity_S(A, x, R)
OwnPolarity_S(B, x, W)
Own_S(C, r)
place(x) = r

Here A owns the read polarity, B owns the write polarity, and C owns the region and the object's resident storage. This does not require A, B, and C to be distinct, but permits them to be. Owning a polarity is not merely having performed an operation or holding a temporary access grant.

A protocol supplies, within the base constraints, construction and transition rules establishing these facts. It may establish different polarity owners or admit a checked partition of existing authority. It determines whether a polarity is uniquely or jointly owned, and which sharing, borrowing, delegation, transfer, recombination, and completion operations are admitted. No general split or duplication law follows from the labels R, W, or RW.

For example, a protocol may permit transferring the write polarity from B to D while A retains the read polarity and C retains region ownership. Such a transition need not change placement or the read polarity's lifetime. Its preconditions and effect on pending operations remain protocol obligations. Neither region ownership nor a write operation alone establishes the authority to create, transfer, or delegate a polarity.

An access protocol derives admitted operations from owned or lawfully delegated polarity authority, its state, and the remaining validity conditions. Owning a polarity can carry responsibilities beyond an individual access, and does not necessarily permit that operation in every protocol state. Conversely, a borrower may have access without owning the polarity. The relationship must be specified rather than recovering ownership from an access trace.

Polarity ownership, any derived grants, and storage protection have related lifetimes, but need not have equal extents. The region must protect every admitted use and required completion justified by either polarity. Ending the write polarity may mean end-of-input while a reader continues; whether that is the protocol's meaning is an explicit choice. It does not by itself reclaim the region. Region reclamation must settle the outstanding polarity and access obligations under the selected composition.

Linearity and uniqueness of polar endpoints

For channels, review usage and alias authority separately for each polar endpoint. Following DN-0038, neither property follows merely from making a handle non-copyable. These are candidate protocol profiles, not new restrictions on current cells or a selection of channel syntax or representation.

Property What it constrains What remains to specify
Linear current-state authority Each current authority value is consumed once by a lawful operation, transfer, or completion path; it cannot be duplicated or silently discarded. Which transitions produce a successor, settle the obligation, or retain it in a pending operation or captured continuation.
Affine current-state authority The current value cannot be used more than once, but may be discarded. Whether a separate owner or an explicit cancellation/completion mechanism must settle the resource when access is discarded.
Unique endpoint authority No incompatible usable alias to the same endpoint authority coexists with the claimed exclusivity. Which aliases may exist but be suspended, which loans are compatible, and how exclusivity is recovered.

Linearity concerns use accounting; uniqueness concerns the authority of aliases. A linear token alone does not prove that a provider issued no conflicting token. Uniqueness alone does not require eventual use, successful communication, or completion. A protocol may combine both properties or use linear permissions to establish its uniqueness invariant.

Let e = (c, p) identify one endpoint role of channel c, and let E(e, q, L) denote its candidate current authority at protocol state q, with validity extent L. An ordinary successful operation can have the schematic form:

step(E(e, q, L), input) -> (E(e, q', L'), output)

The old authority is consumed and its successor carries the next state. This permits arbitrarily many operations when the protocol permits them; linearity does not make the channel a one-message channel. Successors must preserve lifetime and backing constraints. This notation neither extends L implicitly nor selects the failure or terminal outcomes. Those must account for retained, returned, transferred, or settled authority explicitly.

Lightweight Functional Session Types, sections 1.3.1 and 1.3.2 provides a concrete comparison: send returns an updated channel, receive returns a payload and updated channel, and the authors distinguish usage linearity from alias freedom. This supports comparing state succession with uniqueness; it does not supply Ash's authority-construction or backing rules.

Uniqueness must name its boundary. Uniqueness of (c, p) need not exclude another actor owning (c, dual(p)), or a third actor owning the backing region. For the dedicated one-way comparison, the roles can be fixed send and receive polarities, usable by different actors over overlapping lifetimes. For richer session protocols, a stable endpoint role may alternate send and receive actions as q changes. Endpoint identity, the current action's direction, and memory read/write authority therefore need separate descriptions. A unique endpoint also does not establish uniqueness of its payload or reachable graph.

Compare three profiles before choosing a mechanism:

Profile Repeated operations and delegation Main obligation
Linear state succession with exclusive endpoint authority Each operation returns a successor; delegation moves the current value. Establish exclusive issuance and preserve it through context splitting, capture, and handoff; linear use checking alone is insufficient.
Reusable unique authority with checked loans Operations preserve the alias invariant; transfer removes the former holder's usable authority. Track loans and suspended aliases, including across callbacks and pending operations, until uniqueness is recovered.
Shared or asymmetric polar authority A protocol may share one polarity while keeping the other exclusive, or mediate both. Define arbitration, ordering, state evolution, and completion without assuming the exclusive profile's rules.

The first two can describe the same dedicated-channel behavior with different checking mechanisms. The third is a useful later multi-producer or multi-consumer comparison. It need not inherit the same session-state or progress guarantees. Shared authority at an access point that creates fresh sessions also differs from sharing an endpoint of an existing session.

The cited resource pi-calculus offers a related recovery construction: a unique whole-channel assumption can split into affine access and authority that becomes unique after a matching communication consumes that access. Its countdown measures communications, not elapsed time. Applying this idea per endpoint would require a new compatibility and preservation argument; it is not already a polar-endpoint rule, a general loan mechanism, or proof that the required communication eventually occurs.

Endpoint delegation adds obligations beyond ordinary name transmission. At commit, the sender loses the delegated current authority; a buffered transport must account for it in the queued message until the receiver claims it. The complementary endpoint may remain with its existing owner throughout. Failure before commit must specify whether authority is returned or otherwise settled; cancellation after commit must account for queued authority without restoring a usable sender alias. A first exclusive comparison can require no outstanding loan or pending operation on the delegated endpoint. Handover of those states needs an additional contract.

The endpoint's authority, the queued message's storage owner, and the channel's backing owner remain distinct. Ending one endpoint does not release storage still needed by its peer, buffers, loans, waiters, or external completion. Whole-channel reclamation requires settlement of all relevant obligations and the backing owner's authority. Per-endpoint uniqueness alone cannot justify it.

Operation modes and their order

For a fixed view and operation contract, consider the abstract modes:

none = {}
R = {read}
W = {write}
RW = {read, write}

Order modes by set inclusion. Their meet is intersection and join is union, giving a four-element permission lattice with incomparable R and W. This describes operation sets, not source types or a rule for issuing authority. The equation R union W = RW does not let a client manufacture a combined capability. Restricting a view's offered operations does not copy or discharge its ownership, payload, loan, or completion obligations.

Swapping the read and write labels is an involution fixing none and RW. It preserves this order; it is not Boolean complement or an order reversal. Nor is it an operation that converts an actual reader into a writer. Channel duality needs its own interpretation below.

A read permission concerns its specified footprint. It does not prove deep immutability, stable contents across calls, permission to copy a payload, or freedom from metadata effects in the implementation. A write permission does not imply permission to read uninitialized storage, reclaim backing, or transfer its owner. Initialization and disposal of overwritten contents need separate protocol and payload rules.

Permissions depend on time and phase

Here a phase is a state of a chosen access protocol, such as construction, published reading, or reusable storage. The protocol can vary the permissions granted to each participant by phase while ownership and placement stay fixed. Read/write modes remain a separate concept; phase-dependent access is an optional composition, not a requirement that every object changes polarity. Another protocol may admit the same modes throughout its usable lifetime. Phases are not a global read-versus-write switch: different actors can hold different permissions over overlapping lifetimes.

For a fixed execution event set E, client a, and view v, let G(a, v) be a subset of {read, write} cross E. Membership records an operation admitted at an event position, not merely an operation that executed. It describes derived access, not the ownership of the underlying polarity. Source checking must establish the relationship for every admitted execution; this semantic relation is not an assumed computable future-use oracle.

For example, actor A can have read access to view v while actor B has write access to the same footprint:

G(A, v) = {read} cross L_A
G(B, v) = {write} cross L_B
L_A intersect L_B != empty

Overlapping grants need not alternate or end when the other actor receives its grant. Their compatibility is an obligation of the selected access protocol, including visibility and synchronization where required. A protocol can admit both while defining how reads observe updates. Grant coexistence and simultaneous execution of raw memory operations are separate questions. The example supplies neither a general exclusion rule nor an unrestricted shared-memory permission.

The region owner can remain a third actor throughout. Its protection must cover both grants' admitted uses and required completion. The clients can have different grant lifetimes and admitted execution domains while referring to the same placement. Combining their facts must preserve the actor index; it cannot grant RW to either actor merely because both operations are present.

This relation retains correlations that a pair of mode and lifetime summaries can lose. As a separate example, suppose one client's interface permits writes during construction and reads after publication, over different nonempty extents L_build and L_read:

G = ({write} cross L_build) union ({read} cross L_read)
G subset_of {read, write} cross (L_build union L_read)

The inclusion can be strict. Replacing G by the larger rectangle grants unintended reads during construction and writes after publication. Thus taking the union of modes and lifetimes independently is not generally a sound capability join. For fixed identities and E, the full relation domain is a set lattice, but arbitrary unions need not be admissible access contracts.

Descriptive restriction to modes M and lifetime L is G intersect (M cross L). Such restrictions commute and are idempotent by intersection laws. These set facts do not authorize discarding pending loans or required completion, duplicating authority, or widening an existing grant.

For each admitted access, require the operation's permission and protocol phase, initialized storage where needed, valid dependencies, live region protection, an admitted execution domain, and the required visibility or synchronization. A lifetime bound or a mode alone cannot discharge that check. All granted uses of a view remain bounded by its usable lifetime and every required backing region, as in the dependency and borrowing bounds above.

Composition, ownership, and storage

Combining permissions held by clients is not the lattice join. Define a separate partial compatibility relation over clients, overlapping footprints, lifetimes, and protocols. For unmediated ordinary storage, a candidate permits overlapping readers and excludes an overlapping writer against another reader or writer. Proven disjoint fields or buffers may permit independent writes within the same region. This does not require a lock or an exclusive permission for an entire region merely because two objects share placement.

Mediated shared mutation, atomics, and locks need their own operation contracts. A reusable public write handle is not a raw exclusive-storage grant. The candidate rule does not change current cells. Likewise, R union R = R says nothing about whether two live readers exist, whether an access value may be duplicated, or whether all read loans have ended. A separate loan or permission ledger must preserve those facts; no fractional or linear representation is selected here.

Where the protocol admits it, a polarity owner may delegate access without transferring that polarity or region ownership. A writer can therefore be a non-owner of storage and may be a borrower of write authority; a storage owner need not have usable mutation authority during a loan. Reclamation requires its distinct authority and the settlement of outstanding admitted uses. RW adds no ownership or reclamation authority.

Using a read view requires its backing, and returned borrowed views retain their actual backing dependencies. Copying an independent value can remove a dependency; the operation must establish the payload's copy contract. Writing a reference into retained storage introduces dependencies that must cover all subsequently admitted reads and required completion, not just the write event. The current generic cell contract's strict retained resource-owner rule remains stronger than this necessary lifetime condition; clearing later does not relax it. Placement in a longer-lived region cannot repair an expired grant or an unsupported stored dependency.

Execution domains may differ by operation: reading could be admitted on several workers while mutation or reclamation requires home support. A read-only interface still needs the representation and metadata checks from the affinity comparison. Mode attenuation neither relocates bytes nor establishes thread safety; ownership transfer must settle or transfer existing grants under its own rule.

Payload variance and endpoint duality

Under a value-flow convention, reading produces a T and writing accepts a T. For comparison only, suppose A <: B is a safe payload substitution relation and the remaining interface contracts are fixed. Simple producing and accepting interfaces then have the candidate variance relationships:

Source<A> <: Source<B>
Sink<B> <: Sink<A>

A source promises an output; a sink promises to accept an input. An interface offering both generally requires payload invariance. This does not introduce Ash subtyping or lifetime-coercion syntax. Shortening a loan's own lifetime is different from changing a reference lifetime inside a payload that aliases may write. The Rust variance reference is an external comparison: function results and inputs have opposite variance, and a mutable reference is invariant in its referent type while covariant in its own reference lifetime. These rules are not adopted by this note.

For a channel, sending accepts a payload from its caller and receiving produces one. Matching endpoint directions are dual, but the direction does not select the permissions of that payload. A sender may read or transfer an immutable message; sending does not require permission to mutate its bytes. A receiver may obtain a read-only lease or an owned writable region, depending on the transport contract.

At the single-message direction level, use explanatory labels Send(T) and Receive(T) with dual(Send(T)) = Receive(T) and the reverse equation, so dual(dual(p)) = p. Protocol continuations, closure, cancellation, and payload transfer must still match. This is separate from swapping memory-access labels and does not grant both directions to either endpoint. Propositions as Sessions provides an external account of session duality. Its calculus and progress results do not follow from these explanatory endpoint equations.

For a zero-copy pool, the producer can hold construction permission, the consumer a later read lease, and the pool retain storage ownership throughout. Publication must establish initialization and the required visibility while ending incompatible producer access. Reuse waits for reader loans and pending operations, including external completion, rather than merely observing a receive event. These are separate phase transitions from ownership transfer and backing relocation.

Sequential placement inference

Keep validity constraints, placement inference, and optimization separate. The constraints determine which placements are admitted. A heuristic selects a candidate satisfying them. Later optimization may change placement or its physical representation only while preserving the established guarantees and observable behavior, including any exposed allocation or completion effects.

For the one-region-per-scope variant, start with the construction scope's region. Widen toward existing enclosing regions only when required uses cannot fit there. For sequential execution with properly nested scope activations, a useful candidate is the least common ancestor of the construction activation and all required use activations:

candidate_scope(x) = least_common_ancestor(
    construction_scope(x), required_use_scopes(x))
candidate_region(x) = region(candidate_scope(x))

These scopes denote related dynamic activations, not just source locations. Including construction ensures that the candidate region is already available when allocation executes. Required uses include aliases, retained fields, closures, and pending completion obligations justified by contracts, rather than only direct uses or accesses observed in a run. Loops, recursion, and function boundaries require summaries that preserve activation relationships; a syntactic common ancestor alone is not a complete inference algorithm.

For example, the following is schematic control flow, not Ash syntax:

scope P:
    x = scope A:
        construct object
        return object
    scope B:
        use x

The object is constructed inside A but used after A ends, inside its lexical neighbour B. The value reaches B through the binding in P; inference does not change name visibility. If all constraints permit it, inference selects P's region before allocation. P owns that region and therefore the object's storage. No object is first allocated into A and then moved, and neither scope's lifetime is extended.

The candidate still needs live exact allocation authority, compatible region type and construction, dependency validity, and agreement with annotations. In particular, placing a closure in P cannot make a resource owned by A available to a later call in B. Related ordinary value allocations may need joint placement constraints; this does not authorize extending resource owners or automatically promoting every reachable dependency. If no admitted region satisfies the requirements, the use must reject.

Widening required by use belongs to initial placement inference. Additional placement choices for efficiency belong to optimization and need an observation contract. This heuristic is limited to the sequential fragment with nested lifetimes; the general algebra remains the validity constraints and ownership facts above. Suspension can preserve an owning activation after control leaves its body, and later concurrent work can introduce other lifetime relationships. Lexical ancestry alone cannot solve those cases. Their contracts remain open; this heuristic does not replace the model's existing capture obligations.

State and reclamation

A candidate state S records region identities and types, ownership obligations, resident objects, placement, initialized representations, permitted access, roots and references, pending construction, and required completion. This is model state, not a selected runtime representation.

Let Resident(r, S) be the objects still allocated in r. Let Need(r, S) be the subset whose preservation valid ownership, access, or completion requires. Its specification must account for references from other regions, closures, captured control, provider state, and pending cleanup. Region ownership does not by itself require every unreachable object to remain individually live.

Need is a semantic obligation, not an assumed computable oracle. A checker or collector may retain more objects through a conservative approximation; proving that it misses none of the required objects is part of its contract.

A region type K supplies its allowed object reclamation policy. Any early reclamation transition removing objects D must satisfy:

D subset_of Resident(r, S) minus Need(r, S)
Resident(r, S_after) = Resident(r, S) minus D

The type may impose stronger conditions or support no early reclamation. A fixed arena initially uses D = empty. Counting and tracing require their own accounting, roots, cycle, failure, and possible reference-update obligations. Neither policy changes region ownership or lifetime merely by being selected.

Whole-region reclamation is a separate transition, authorized by the ownership scheme after remaining permitted uses end and required completion no longer needs the storage. It consumes reclamation authority once and establishes:

Resident(r, S_after) = empty
no permitted access remains to an object allocated in r

All remaining objects are reclaimed together, including ordinary cycles that an earlier policy retained. Objects merely referenced in another region are outside this reclamation. Ending lexical source access and consuming a physical owner remain distinct: a retained dependent can block backing release after ordinary access has been revoked. The model must preserve that protection.

Ownership contexts and typed transitions

Let Delta be an ordered context of ownership and completion obligations. Compatible concatenation Delta tensor Gamma preserves exact identities and dependency order, with no duplicated completion authority. I is empty. Reassociation changes grouping, not order or scope boundaries:

(Delta tensor Gamma) tensor Theta
    = Delta tensor (Gamma tensor Theta)
I tensor Delta = Delta = Delta tensor I

These structural equalities follow from concatenation, conditional on complete context compatibility. They do not justify exchange, owner duplication, scope fusion, or merging region identities. Reusable handles remain separate from these restricted obligations.

Use C : Delta0 -> Delta1 for a model computation transforming a checked state under one ownership context into another. Compatible transitions sequence only when their intermediate state and ownership contracts agree. For finite paths, relation composition gives associativity:

(f ; g) ; h = f ; (g ; h)

Failure outcomes, committed effects, and ordered traces must be included in the eventual computation model. This regrouping must not restart or undo an earlier operation. DN-0032 supplies the bounded sequencing comparison; a concrete region calculus must still establish its typing and operational laws.

Sequential processes and dedicated channels

This future comparison composes sequential processes with isolated local regions. Begin with a dedicated unidirectional channel c connecting one producer P and one consumer Q, with a fixed message type and payload contract. Distinguish synchronous rendezvous from a bounded FIFO buffer; neither interpretation is implied by the word channel. Endpoint delegation, multiple producers or consumers, shared mutation, and a multicore memory model remain outside this case. No scheduling API is selected.

Network composition and communication order

Write P parallel_c Q for the network with that connection. This composition combines process states, channel state, pending operations, and ownership facts. It is distinct from ordered ownership-context concatenation tensor; the channel connects obligations belonging to different process activations.

Let a precedes b be the transitive closure of each process's event order and the matching communication order. For buffered delivery, publication precedes claim by the receiver. For rendezvous, both processes participate in one handoff event. Communication orders the relevant events; it does not establish that one process's entire lifetime contains the other's. Thus lexical common ancestry alone cannot determine message placement.

Regrouping a network is a candidate structural law only when it preserves the same process identities, channel connections, capacities, and observations. It does not reorder events within a process or imply a scheduling policy. Commuting independent local steps would need separate premises about disjoint support and observations; shared allocation capacity can invalidate that law.

For a buffered channel without message discards, record sequences of distinct message identities in publication order, receive-commit order, and the queue. The candidate FIFO invariant is:

published(c) = received(c) ++ queued(c)
0 <= length(queued(c)) <= capacity(c)

Here ++ is sequence concatenation. It has an empty identity and associative grouping, but does not commute. These elementary sequence laws do not permit arbitrary concatenated executions: capacity, closed state, and message ownership still constrain every prefix. A discard policy needs an explicit disposition record instead of silently deleting a message from this accounting. Rendezvous has no persistent queue; it requires a matching participant, rather than being an ordinary buffered send into a permanently full zero-capacity queue.

Payload contracts

Dedicated endpoints do not by themselves establish a message's storage or access permissions. Compare three contracts explicitly:

Contract Ownership and placement Additional obligation
Copy data Construct a fresh message graph in independently owned storage. The source graph retains its placement and owner. Specify copying of sharing and cycles, permitted payloads, observations, failure, and costs. A copied local resource handle still depends on its original owner and is not independent data.
Transfer a message region Move responsibility for one complete region and its resident objects; placement and region identity remain unchanged. Establish transferable backing and completion support, no remaining sender or third-party access, and no unsupported external dependencies.
Share storage Keep the original region owner and grant specified cross-process access. Prove the shared lifetime and access/visibility contract. Immutability or a long lifetime alone does not establish safe sharing. Excluded from the first isolated comparison.

Copying data is the initial isolated-storage baseline from DN-0035. Region transfer is a second ownership scheme to compare algebraically, not an implied optimization of copying: object identity, failure, and storage observations may distinguish them. Begin either comparison with data-only payloads; transporting resource handles, handlers, endpoints, or captured control needs further rules.

Transferring one reference into a general process region cannot transfer that object's storage ownership independently. Region ownership covers all resident objects. Either copy the message into independent storage, or prepare a whole detachable message region. Splitting regions is not assumed. A unique completion owner is insufficient evidence that sender aliases or hidden captures are gone.

Ownership during delivery

Use Own_S(o, r) for ownership in state S. The following is an ownership history for successful buffered transfer of a prepared message region r_m; it is not an unconditional operational rule:

Own_S0(P, r_m)
    -- publication commits --> Own_S1(c, r_m)
    -- receive commits -----> Own_S2(Q, r_m)

Here c denotes the channel's obligation for accepted undelivered messages, not either endpoint's observation handle. A surrounding owner must preserve channel support until accepted messages, waiters, and cleanup are settled. The receiver can retain the accepted region after the queue slot is reused.

Each successful handoff removes the old ownership fact and establishes the new one together. Placement, region type, and internal reclamation policy remain unchanged; future collection must use the new owner's roots. On a synchronous rendezvous, ownership passes directly from P to Q in the matched event, without a persistently queued message owner. Copying first creates a distinct message region under a construction owner; publishing that region follows the same delivery obligations while the source graph remains owned by P.

For every unreclaimed message region, the candidate invariant is:

|{ o : Own_S(o, r_m) }| = 1
required_use(m) <= valid(m) <= region_lifetime(r_m)

Every live state must have an owner protecting the region, including periods with no running user. Its lifetime must cover construction, pending delivery, receiver use, and required completion. It need not equal either process's whole lifetime. This transportable-region scheme replaces the lexical lifetime equality for those regions; it does not silently extend a region still bound to an ordinary lexical owner. The owning obligation must be detachable from the construction scope under an explicit rule before that scope can complete.

For transfer, all resident storage and closing obligations must be eligible, and all remaining sender access must be ruled out. A closed ordinary object graph may include internal cycles. References to sender-local resources, foreign thread-affine support, or other non-transferable dependencies reject unless a separate contract establishes their transport. Changing Own_S alone does not establish access revocation, initialized data, or cross-worker visibility. Those remain premises of a future handoff rule.

Zero-copy variations

Specify the boundary of a zero-copy promise. Here the candidate promise is no additional payload copying during channel publication and receipt. Initial construction, descriptor copying, allocation, synchronization, physical page movement, and remote access costs are separate observations. An end-to-end I/O promise needs a larger boundary. A guaranteed zero-copy operation cannot silently fall back to copying; an optional fast path needs its own contract.

Compare these variations without conflating storage ownership with temporary access. The borrowing, pooling, and sharing rows extend the isolated baseline only under additional contracts:

Variation Placement and ownership Conditions to compare
Copy to independent storage A fresh message region follows the delivery history above. Choose its backing policy separately. Baseline payload-copy cost, failure, graph/identity observations, and receiver locality.
Transfer a prepared region The complete region changes owner; payload storage stays in place. No remaining sender access; admissible use and reclamation on the destination or through retained support.
Construct directly in final storage The producer initializes reserved receiver- or channel-supported storage; publication changes access phase. A checked construction permit, initialized publication, and precise responsibility for refusal, partial construction, or cancellation. No existing payload is copied.
Borrow a view Storage ownership remains with the lender; the receiver gets a bounded lease. The region covers the lease; incompatible mutation and reclamation wait for all admitted uses and acknowledgements. Dedicated endpoints alone do not prove this.
Reuse channel or pool slots A stable region owner retains storage while write, queued, read, and reusable phases govern a slot. Receipt does not imply the consumer finished using the bytes. Reuse waits for every view and pending operation; stale accesses must not become valid for a later slot generation.
Share immutable data An enclosing or separately specified shared owner retains storage. Cross-process lifetime, roots, representation safety, and reclamation accounting. This is a sharing contract rather than region-owner transfer.
Send descriptors for several buffers A message names slices of one or more existing regions. Every backing dependency remains protected. Descriptor transfer alone neither transfers those regions nor makes sender-local backing independent.
Use registered I/O or device buffers A region provides storage with an external operation in flight. Registration, layout, address stability, and completion acknowledgement protect bytes until the external user has finished, including cancellation races. No device contract is assumed here.

For a borrowed view v, let backing_regions(v) include every region needed for its descriptor and referenced bytes. Necessary lifetime bounds include:

lease_use(v) <= valid(v)
valid(v) <= intersection(region_lifetime(r) for r in backing_regions(v))

These bounds do not establish mutation exclusion or synchronization; the earlier resource-dependency bounds still apply. A pool can remain the storage owner throughout; the producer's construction permission and consumer's read permission are separate phase facts. Ending a queued entry therefore differs from ending payload access and from reclaiming its region. Per-slot reuse is not an assumed region split or a transfer of storage ownership.

The process comparison concerns logical Ash processes. Same-address-space access, separate address spaces, and remote machines require different transport contracts. A pointer or ownership fact alone does not establish a valid mapping or representation at the destination.

Placement and execution affinity

Thread/processor alignment here means execution affinity and memory locality. Keep byte alignment as a separate construction and access constraint. Extend the model with distinct facts:

Fact Candidate meaning
Own_S(o, r) Who holds the region's ownership and completion obligation now.
place(x) = r Which logical region contains the object.
backing_location_S(r) Where backing is resident, potentially across several memory nodes; allocation policy and actual residence are distinct.
execution_domain_S(P) The process's current worker/thread/processor placement, with no assumption that these identities coincide.
allowed_domains(r, op, S) Domains allowed to perform a particular operation with its transitive representation, provider, resource, and completion support.

For an event e operating on r, admission requires its execution domain to belong to the operation's allowed domains, in addition to ownership, access, initialization, lifetime, and visibility obligations. Allowed-domain sets can be intersected when several supports constrain one operation; an empty intersection rejects that execution choice. This does not order complete ownership schemes or prove that a legal domain is efficient.

Changing process placement, transferring region ownership, and relocating backing are three distinct transitions. None implies either of the others. Migration must account for retained support and later completion, not just the next payload read. Compare same-worker execution, fixed per-core processes, and migration among an admitted domain set without selecting one scheduler.

Hard restrictions and preferences also differ. A required thread for a foreign operation or reclamation is an admission condition. Preferred processor/cache or memory-node locality is a cost policy unless the contract makes it a hard restriction. Byte, cache-line, page, and device alignment requirements must state which are required for valid access and which are performance choices. No universal alignment size or platform topology is selected.

Zero-copy ownership transfer need not produce receiver-local storage. Compare leaving backing in place, constructing with a destination-oriented allocation policy, copying to different backing, moving execution toward the data, and explicit backing migration under its own stability contract. Measure payload copies, retained bytes, metadata and synchronization work, remote accesses, and completion traffic; zero copied payload bytes alone is not a cost order.

Reclamation may be restricted to a home worker even when remote payload access is admitted. The receiving owner can then retain a unique completion obligation and route reclamation through home support. If that obligation is delegated to a completion record, the transfer must preserve its uniqueness until settlement. Protect home support and backing while a return or acknowledgement is pending; neither consumer completion nor a posted reclamation request proves release.

These distinctions have external precedents, not Ash implementation evidence. Seastar's multicore contract uses per-core services and routes destruction of a moved foreign_ptr back to its originating core. Linux documents distinct CPU and memory-node restrictions and NUMA allocation policy; changing allocation policy does not by itself relocate existing pages. They motivate separate access, placement, and reclamation facts; they do not select an Ash runtime or prove any candidate transition above.

Failure, closure, and reclamation

The publication and receive commits determine ownership even if notification or process resumption happens later. Full, closed, or cancelled send before publication leaves the prepared message with its sender or pending-operation owner. After publication, sender cancellation cannot silently take it back. Cancellation before receive commitment leaves the queued owner unchanged; after commitment, the receiver or its completion path must settle the region. A partially constructed copy must close its own obligations without publishing readable data or consuming the original graph.

Closing admission is distinct from draining or discarding accepted messages. Choose an explicit policy for queued messages and pending sends when an endpoint closes or a process fails. The invariant requires every outstanding region to remain owned until delivery, an explicit return, or authorized completion and reclamation. Receiver failure cannot orphan an accepted message; sender exit cannot invalidate one already transferred. Final channel reclamation waits for its remaining support obligations. It cannot reclaim a region already received by Q merely because that region travelled through the channel.

Queue entries, pending sends, and completion paths are roots for Need(r, S). No collector may treat a message as dead merely because neither process is currently reading it. Reclaiming a transferred region still reclaims all its remaining objects en masse after required completion. Queue capacity bounds accepted entries, not message bytes, blocked producers, receiver-retained data, or total protected backing; those need separate bounds.

The proof target is global validity preservation from checked process steps and checked communication transitions, including publication, receipt, cancellation, and closure. Unique ownership and lifetime coverage are safety obligations. They do not prove deadlock freedom, fairness, eventual receipt, eventual reclamation, or a bound on retained memory. Those need separate protocol, scheduling, and capacity premises.

Evidence and experiments

The first proof target is preservation of well-formed state and required object validity across each admitted transition. A finite execution then preserves the invariant by induction, provided every transition case has been proved. The allocation, initialization, root, dependency, and completion premises needed for that argument remain to be specified and discharged.

The fixed-arena case is the first bounded model. Compare a collector only after specifying its root and reclamation contract; current scalar Arena evidence does not prove these graph, lifetime, or collection cases. Record contrary cases as well as successful derivations. No new executable or machine-checked proof has been produced for this note.

Channel cases are future comparisons, not evidence for the first sequential model. A later bounded assignment must specify copy or transfer payloads, rendezvous or buffering, commit points, endpoint closure, cancellation, and the observations used to compare network executions before claiming a proof. For a zero-copy or affinity comparison, also name the exact copy boundary, access phases, admitted domains, backing policy, and reclamation route.

The literature comparisons supply additional positive and contrary cases, including name extrusion, I/O capability restriction, delegated cancellation, captured linear authority, and shared access points that change progress guarantees. They are evidence about their stated fragments, not experimental results or correctness evidence for Ash.

A later prototype needs a separately bounded assignment naming the state and operation fragment, observations, comparison policies, and retention conditions. Retain reproducible proofs, countermodels, and measured policy differences as design evidence. Discard machinery that silently extends owners, hides backing dependencies, or assumes a runtime representation to make an algebraic law true. Product adoption requires the proposal or decision and task workflow.

  • Which support facts constrain access, execution migration, and reclamation domains independently, and how is home support retained through shutdown?
  • Which locality and copy-cost observations guide policy without silently changing lifetime, alignment, failure, or visibility contracts?
  • Which permission and loan facts preserve client, footprint, mode, and lifetime correlations through abstraction without inventing new grants at a join?
  • Which protocol rules establish read and write polarity ownership, and permit its partition, sharing, borrowing, or transfer independently of storage ownership? What responsibilities and completion obligations does each carry?
  • For each endpoint, which rules concern linear or affine use accounting and which concern unique alias authority? Can linear succession and reusable unique access preserve the same observable protocol and failure behavior?
  • What checked construction establishes exclusive authority initially, and how can loans, delegation, capture, or protocol transitions recover or transfer it without forbidding the complementary endpoint's concurrent use?
  • Which raw and mediated access protocols admit compatible aliases, and how do initialization, publication, retained writes, and reuse update those facts?
  • Which payload variance and endpoint-duality relationships survive the chosen effects, usage, lifetime, and transport contracts without conflating them?

Further algebra work must identify which state, context-compatibility and transition premises differ from the specified lexical base, and keep its additional proof obligations separate from the retained bounded model. This note authorizes no such work. Placement inference and comparisons with another reclamation or ownership scheme remain future questions; they do not redefine the product's default placement or enlarge the existing proof claim. The dedicated-channel case supplies a later comparison: start with copied, independent data, then compare transportable regions under the same message protocol. Preserve the separation between memory safety and progress claims.
