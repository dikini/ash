DN-0034: Verification Foundation for Ash Semantics and Implementation

Development design notes · ash-lang 9a8b73b

Informative design work. Proposals and experiments may be incomplete or superseded. Read the language specification for current rules.

All design notes
  • ID: DN-0034
  • Status: evaluating
  • Related: DN-0033, DN-0031, DN-0032

This note assesses a verification direction. It defines no current Ash semantics, accepts no architecture, and authorizes no implementation or new project policy. Tooling and workflow recommendations below remain candidates.

Question

How can the one-shot formalization become a maintainable foundation for proving both the soundness of existing and proposed Ash operational semantics and the correctness of their implementation, while language development continues?

The one-shot fragment is an intended base for extension. The goal is broader than publishing proofs or extracting a standalone continuation checker. It is to connect specified behavior, formal semantics, safety properties, and actual implementation behavior, then preserve those connections through changes.

Context and evidence boundary

The specification defines current Ash. Its handler and completion rules and static semantics govern exact dispatch, one-shot non-escape, checked discard, resource support, and cleanup obligations. The Core decision requires typed structure and independent validation; the evaluator decision supplies the explicit-machine boundary. DN-0033 supplies candidate semantics and proof experiments, with its evidence register and mathematical companion.

As inspected on 2026-09-30, the current annotated-state M0–M4 boundary includes structural resource admission, all 32 transition families, initial admission, constructive preservation, progress under the admitted builtin-signature law, and bounded execution from an admitted program. The operative statements are initial admission and M4. Earlier sections of the large evidence documents describe narrower or superseded proof boundaries; counts of historical open items do not measure current completion.

These results require the existing structural certificates. They establish source-selected preserving execution, not preservation for arbitrary raw annotation choices. Initial admission means experimental calculus input, not an automatic translation of every accepted Ash program. Finite-prefix construction does not prove termination or eventual release during divergence.

Core alignment is active separately on codex/core-alignment. This assessment uses its WIP snapshot b940171, including TASK-0032 at docs/tasks/TASK-0032-core-one-shot-alignment.md and comparison evidence at docs/design/direct-effects/one-shot/CORE-ALIGNMENT.md on that branch. These records are branch-local evidence, not integrated product truth. They target static admission from independently validated Core and execution correspondence for a declared monomorphic fragment containing handlers, requests, sequencing, resume, discard, cells, cleanup, and supporting ordinary constructs.

The recorded comparison has already produced useful results: the direct versus bound resume-operand discrepancy led to CP-0021 and locally verified regressions; constructing full-fixture admission exposed continuation-interface coherence and declaration-recipe gaps in the proof model. Exported Core, an independent Core machine, value relations, and selected transition proofs provide bridge components. General admission, configuration simulation, and the integrated correspondence theorem remain unfinished. These findings support continued investment without establishing correctness for all implemented Ash.

Assessment

Soundness, correctness, and intended behavior

Semantic soundness concerns the formal language: admitted programs preserve types, interpretation requirements, ownership and completion obligations, and can advance or reach a permitted outcome. The theorem must state its admission rules and environmental assumptions. Safety does not imply termination.

Implementation correctness concerns correspondence with those semantics. Compilation must preserve intended behavior, and implementation transitions must have justified semantic matches. Administrative steps need a progress argument when they match no semantic step. Observations must include relevant results, effects, ownership events, failures, and divergence; the direction and strength of the relation must be explicit.

Correctness of a proposed semantic change also needs stated intended laws and observations. A sound calculus can specify an unwanted cleanup order. Examples, observable properties, and specification review establish intent; safety and correspondence proofs answer different questions.

The eventual chain includes surface elaboration, validated Core, execution, and external boundaries. A theorem about a Lean model of Core execution still needs an explicit justification for its connection to the Rust implementation. Exported inputs and matching traces are useful evidence but do not prove that connection for every program or execution.

Practical value and maintenance risk

The formalization addresses actual Ash interactions: requests capture mixed handler, resource, and unfinished clause control. Shared ownership and completion invariants explain why a local result type or effect row alone does not suffice. Current comparison findings show practical value through defect classification, semantic clarification, and regression cases.

The alignment effort turns that value into a concrete verification programme. Its admission and simulation boundaries are more useful near-term priorities than speculative standalone analyzers. For example, Usage.lean checks local consumption under supplied rights and explicitly excludes capture compatibility and resource-safe disposal; it is not already an Ash safety checker.

Maintainability remains an open assessment. File count, theorem count, and completed-case percentages do not establish usability or predict maintenance cost. Useful evidence is whether contributors can find assumptions, extend a construct, repair affected proofs, and reproduce a discrepancy without reconstructing the entire experiment history.

Parallel development and extension pressure

Language features, proof coverage, principled FFI, surface DX, and generated property tests can advance concurrently. Their shared coordination boundary is the behavior and invariants changed by each increment, together with the exact revision and fragment to which a proof or comparison applies.

Effort Connection to the verification foundation
New Ash features State the semantic delta and changed invariants; retain positive and negative evidence and mark correspondence coverage.
Broader Lean coverage Extend admission, execution and correspondence together where possible; distinguish model extension from coverage of implemented behavior.
Multishot and escaping continuations Revisit duplication, lifetime, transfer and completion assumptions explicitly. Reuse one-shot results only where their premises still hold.
Discard and abandonment extensions Preserve structural closing order, surviving interpreter requirements and running completion protection. Current one-shot discard already provides a base.
Principled FFI Express provider contracts using resources and effects where sufficient; state foreign lifetime, aliasing, callback, failure and concurrency assumptions. Distinguish assumed contracts from verified foreign implementations.
Surface DX Relate convenient syntax and elaboration to Core behavior; test diagnostics and rejection as well as successful execution.
Tuned property tests Use laws, model/implementation agreement and expected rejection as explicit oracles; retain seeds and minimized counterexamples.

The candidate workflow permits features to advance before complete proof coverage, with the gap recorded and owned. It does not weaken existing product verification or mark an unsupported feature verified. A change to a shared verified invariant needs an assessment of affected guarantees. Independent changes should not wait for unrelated proofs or a full-language checkpoint.

Tooling priorities

Build on existing checks and alignment components. Each additional tool should remove a demonstrated repeated cost or close a specific evidence gap.

Capability Useful result and boundary
Reproducible proof checks Pin the toolchain, check affected dependency closures, audit theorem dependencies and axioms, and retain source revision and build provenance. Cache reuse must authenticate inputs.
Verified-coverage navigation Connect constructs and interactions to theorem entry points, implementation boundaries, assumptions, exclusions and regressions. Generate dependency details from code where possible.
Core export and execution comparison Reuse actual validated Core and explicit observations to expose discrepancies. Keep exporter, model and observer limitations visible.
Generation and shrinking Generate admitted cases and targeted invalid cases, compare against explicit oracles, and shrink while preserving the relevant typing or control interaction.
Change-impact assistance Identify affected imports, theorem interfaces, rules and fixtures; begin with compiler dependencies and maintained links before building a separate index.

Property generation can be biased by coverage, rare control interactions, or past failures. Compare tuning strategies using useful discrepancies found, reproducibility, and shrinking quality. Coverage is an exploration signal, not a correctness oracle. Generated passing tests supplement proofs.

Standalone analyzers, dashboards, automatic proof adaptation, and skeleton proof generation remain possible later tools. They need a demonstrated user workflow and trustworthy input boundary. They are not prerequisites for continuing Core alignment or language development.

Documentation and project artifacts

Use the existing artifact lifecycle and documentation policy. The candidate below adds information at its natural owner rather than a second approval pipeline.

Information needed Smallest suitable artifact
Current language behavior Existing spec/ chapters; formal evidence links must not replace their authority.
Proposed semantic delta and required evidence Existing change proposal, including affected invariants and verification coverage when relevant.
Uncertain rules or proof experiments A design note with a bounded question, comparison criteria and retention conditions.
Accepted verification architecture or trusted boundary An architectural decision once exploration converges. Routine lemma and test changes need no new decision.
Work ownership, dependencies and remaining gaps Existing active task; use an execution plan only when sequencing is complicated enough to justify one.
Connected verification guarantees A compact current coverage section in the proof README, linking theorem endpoints, supported fragments, assumptions, implementation evidence and exclusions.
Reproducible discrepancies Executable fixtures plus brief classification in the owning task or evidence record. Keep explanations beside the evidence.
Contributor guidance One concise guide in the proof directory, linked from its README; split it only when it becomes difficult to use.

The coverage section should distinguish proved, experimentally compared, unsupported, and invalidated claims. Record the revision for comparisons that have not been re-established on the current tree. Keep test-only exporters and observers distinguishable from mechanized implementation guarantees. Reuse existing evidence registers and avoid a manually maintained theorem catalogue.

The contributor guide should explain the verification contract and trusted boundary, existing check commands, theorem/module conventions, and one worked semantic extension. Include a short discrepancy recipe: reproduce, minimize, classify, locate authority, repair, and recheck. Link detailed premises from Lean rather than duplicating them in prose.

A new permanent artifact is justified when it owns information that cannot be kept clearly in these existing places. This note recommends the coverage section and guide as maintenance aids; it does not create new gates or require a separate verification proposal, readiness checklist, or coordination report for every feature.

Reducing coordination friction

Keep each fact at one owner. Proposals explain semantic changes; tasks track work; evidence records explain results; theorem statements carry premises. Cross-link those owners instead of copying status into several documents. Automate counts, dependency graphs and check results where they are useful.

Use a short verification-impact paragraph in an existing proposal or task: changed behavior or invariants, affected proof boundary, relevant checks, and any remaining gap with its owner. Omit it when the change has no verification impact. Review those facts in the existing review rather than scheduling a separate handoff ceremony.

Use focused checks while iterating and the governing integration checks at completion. Publish stable theorem interfaces for shared work; coordinate changes to those interfaces directly. Parallel branches can pin a common baseline and exchange small fixtures or lemmas without waiting for every workstream to converge. Keep execution plans temporary and remove completed transition artifacts according to the existing lifecycle.

Module organization

Provide feature-oriented entry points for handlers, resources, cleanup and continuation operations. Organize Lean internals by dependencies and stable boundaries such as admission, shared invariants, runtime relations and simulation. Substitution, ownership accounting and transport cross features; forcing them into separate feature silos can duplicate proofs.

Restructure when changes repeatedly touch unrelated layers, duplicate machinery or require excessive context. WIP status permits useful refactoring, but a large file count alone does not justify reorganizing the entire library.

Recommendation and evaluation criteria

Continue developing the one-shot foundation and the active Core alignment bridge. Make its verified boundary and assumptions easy to inspect, then extend coverage through concrete language work. Introduce tooling and guidance incrementally where they make that work cheaper or its guarantees clearer.

A useful next evaluation is a real semantic extension carried through intended laws, operational rules, admission, implementation, regression evidence and correspondence. Compare the context needed, changed proof surface, check cost, and defects or missing assumptions discovered. Retain experimental tooling when it repeatedly improves that workflow; simplify or discard it when its upkeep exceeds its benefit. No additional prototype is authorized by this note.

Open questions

  • Which reusable admission and simulation interfaces emerge from Core alignment?
  • What claim can be established between the Lean Core machine and the Rust evaluator, and which implementation components remain trusted?
  • Which observations and environmental assumptions are required for failures, divergence and foreign providers?
  • Which one-shot invariants survive duplication and escape, and which require new contracts or proofs?
  • How much proof lag is acceptable for a feature, and which existing guarantees must be re-established before its integration?
  • Which generation and shrinking approach finds useful interaction failures at acceptable maintenance cost?
  • Does the proposed coverage section and contributor guide remove enough repeated context reconstruction, or can existing material be simplified first?
